Art & Color

The covers are a fictional educational series from the late 1960s and early 1970s: one publisher, a different designer for each lesson. Color says which shelf a class is on; artwork says which class it is.

Where the rules live. This page is the color system and the look. The exact lines that go into a cover prompt, and the prompt format itself, are on Cover Prompts.

How the Colors Work

Every Shelf Has a Color Pair

Each shelf is identified by two colors working together: a ground (the background field) and a title color (the big type at upper left). The pair is the shelf’s identity. Every class on a shelf inherits it: Reggae, Ska and Mento all look like Caribbean; Bebop and Jazz Fusion all look like Jazz. The ground says which shelf you are on; the artwork says which class you are looking at.

Shelves Can Share a Ground

Two shelves may use the same ground as long as their title colors are clearly different, which lets the system grow without running out of distinct colors.

When a shelf relies on its title color to stand apart, that color also appears in its artwork, so the shelf still reads at thumbnail size. Metal art carries blood red. Hip Hop art carries gold. Punk art carries citron.

Two Default Type Colors

Cream #EDE4D1 for dark grounds and warm ink #29251F for light ones. Neither is pure white or pure black. Accent title colors (oxblood, blood red, citron, gold, teal) are used only where they pass contrast at 4:1 or better and help a shared ground stay distinct.

Light and Dark Are Mixed on Purpose

Neighboring shelves alternate between light and dark grounds where possible, so a row of covers never turns into one block of the same value. On light grounds, artwork uses darker inks so it still reads.

The Palette

The colors are chosen the way a 1980s art director would spec them from a swatch book: slightly grayed, rich but not neon. Light grounds behave like paper stocks. No two shelves should look alike at thumbnail size. Prompts say rich ink ground, texture must not wash out the color.

Cover Composition

Use one visual idea that expresses the class’s listening revelation. Work in a square, with large flat forms, strong negative space, restrained typography and a limited period palette. Look at neighboring covers before repeating a device. Let composition distinguish the covers, even when they share a ground color.

Type and Print

Keep the title and subtitle in a consistent upper-left footprint. Use a heavy condensed uppercase title and a bold, readable subtitle. Leave clear space above the artwork and keep lettering legible at thumbnail size. Use subtle print grain and slight ink wear. If a brief calls for a worn sleeve, keep the frayed edge thin and the artwork full bleed.
